Negotiate Your Working Capital Peg at the LOI

The price on your letter of intent assumes a normal level of working capital in the business on closing day. Who defines "normal," and when, decides how much of that price reaches your bank account.

By Martin PavlovicM&A, sell-side7 min read

In SRS Acquiom's data, almost every private-target M&A deal now ends with a working capital true-up, and most of those true-ups move money after closing. The firm's 2026 M&A Working Capital PPA Study covered more than 1,570 private deals. It found that 91% carried a purchase price adjustment, and 89% of those adjustments ended in a payment. More of those payments went to buyers than to sellers.

The working capital peg decides which way the check goes. You have the most say over it before you sign the LOI (letter of intent). After you grant a buyer exclusivity, the peg's definition gets written during documentation, often by the buyer's counsel, at a point when you've stopped talking to anyone else.

If you've read our post on why deal structure matters more than valuation, this is the same lesson applied to a line that's easy to skim. Owners spend weeks on the headline multiple, yet on a $20M deal the peg can move $200,000 or more. Fixing it costs little. You settle the method, the definitions and the timing in the LOI, while a second buyer still matters.

What the working capital peg is

Most deals are priced cash-free, debt-free. You keep the cash, you pay off the debt, and the buyer gets a business that can run on day one without writing an extra check to fund it. Working capital is the money tied up in running it day to day.

Net working capital (NWC) in a deal means current assets minus current liabilities, with cash and debt taken out. In practice that's mostly receivables, inventory and prepaid expenses, minus payables and accrued expenses. The deal definition and your accountant's balance sheet often differ, and that difference is where the arguments start.

The peg is the level of NWC you and the buyer agree the business normally needs. At closing, your actual NWC is measured against it. If you deliver less, the price drops dollar for dollar. If you deliver more, the price usually rises by the difference.

So a $200,000 gap between the peg and your closing balance sheet is a $200,000 change in your proceeds. No multiple applies, and your EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) never enters the math.

How often the working capital peg costs you

The typical adjustment is small. In the SRS Acquiom data, final buyer claims averaged 0.76% of transaction value, with a median of 0.27%. If you stopped reading there, you'd file the peg under paperwork.

Two other findings change that picture. The direction tilts toward buyers. Of final outcomes, 51% were buyer claims, 38% were seller surpluses, and 11% had no adjustment. The tail is also fat. About 20% of final claims equaled or exceeded 1% of transaction value. On a $20M sale, 1% is $200,000 coming back out of your proceeds after you thought the deal was done.

The money also sits out of reach for a while. In the first three quarters of 2025, 75% of deals with an adjustment clause set up a separate escrow for the adjustment, up from 69% in 2020. That cash is held by a third party for months after closing, and it's yours only once the true-up settles.

How the working capital peg gets set

The usual starting point is a trailing 12-month (TTM) average. You take NWC at each of the last 12 month-ends and average them. Buyers sometimes propose a 3- or 6-month window instead, especially for a fast-growing company, where last year's average can understate what the business needs today.

Each method favors someone. A short window that ends in your busiest season sets a high peg. A TTM average smooths that out, but it can still cost you if you close in your slowest month.

Take a home services company (the numbers here are illustrative, not from a real deal). Its NWC climbs to $1.6M in summer, when installation receivables pile up, and falls to $1.0M in winter. Its TTM average is $1.3M. The deal closes in January, with $1.0M of NWC on the balance sheet.

Peg method Peg Closing NWC You give back
3 months ending in August $1.6M $1.0M $600,000
TTM average $1.3M $1.0M $300,000
January average of the prior 2 years $1.05M $1.0M $50,000

The business and its January balance sheet are identical in all three rows, so the method alone moves $550,000.

Growth works the same way. If revenue climbed fast, receivables grew with it, and the buyer can fairly argue for a more recent window. Have that trend charted before the buyer raises it.

Where the number moves after you sign

Agreeing on a method settles only part of the peg. The definitions around it can still move what you pay.

The line-item definition

Which accounts count is a negotiation. A buyer can argue that receivables over 90 days or slow-moving inventory should come out of current assets. They can also argue that payroll accruals or deferred revenue are debt-like items you pay off at closing. Each reclassification moves money, and if the buyer drafts the definition after diligence, they draft it with their diligence findings in hand.

Accounting policies

A peg calculated under your historical accounting and a closing balance sheet measured under the buyer's post-diligence policies can disagree by a lot. A new bad-debt reserve or a bigger inventory write-down lowers your closing NWC while nothing in the business has changed. Ask for the closing balance sheet to follow the same policies used to set the peg, listed in a schedule. A sell-side quality of earnings review earns its fee here, because it surfaces the reserve and accrual questions before a buyer's team does.

Who prepares the closing statement, and when

The buyer usually prepares the final statement. In the SRS data the median deadline was about 90 days after closing, and sellers generally got 30 days to review it. Buyers often run late, and 32% of proposed final calculations arrived after the contractual deadline. Some agreements make the buyer's numbers final if you don't object in time. You want your review clock to start when you have the full workpapers in hand.

The dispute clause

About 29% of deals with an adjustment clause dispute the buyer's first calculation, and SRS reports that disputed adjustments almost always end well for sellers. So keep a clean path to a dispute. Name the independent accountant, limit their review to the disputed items, and agree how their fees get split.

Working capital peg terms to settle in the LOI

In most deals the LOI isn't binding on price, but what it says becomes the starting point for the purchase agreement. Ask for these terms in writing before you grant exclusivity:

  1. The methodology. Name it: a TTM monthly average, or a seasonal method that matches your closing month if your business swings.
  2. The line items. List what's included and excluded, including how deferred revenue and payroll accruals are treated.
  3. Accounting policies. The closing balance sheet follows the same policies used to calculate the peg.
  4. A preliminary peg. A number, or at least a range, built from your own monthly balance sheets.
  5. Timing. When the buyer delivers the closing statement, how long you have to review it, and access to the workpapers.
  6. Disputes. An independent accountant, reviewing only the disputed items.
  7. Escrow. Its size, and whether a shortfall comes out of that escrow only. In the SRS data the separate escrow covered a median of only a third of the final claim, so ask where the rest would come from.

A buyer's team negotiates these terms on deal after deal, and you'll probably sell once. The LOI, while you still have other buyers, is the cheapest place to make up that difference in experience.

What to do before you go to market

Start with at least 12 months of monthly balance sheets that close on time and use consistent accounting. Without them, you can't calculate a credible TTM average, and the buyer's team will build the number for you. If you plan to argue for a seasonal method like the January average above, you'll need 24 months. If your close process isn't there yet, our guide to getting your books exit-ready covers the cleanup.

Then calculate your own peg under 2 or 3 methods, so you know which one a buyer's proposal favors before it arrives. With the financials buyers look for already in order, that's an afternoon's work for your controller, done before any buyer has exclusivity.
